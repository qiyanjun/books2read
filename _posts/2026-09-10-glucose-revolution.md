# Title: Glucose Revolution: The Life-Changing Power of Balancing Your Blood Sugar
- by Jessie Inchauspé
- [URL](https://www.amazon.com/Glucose-Revolution-Life-Changing-Power-Balancing/dp/B09CW98LZF)

- Summary: Every time you eat a croissant on an empty stomach, your blood sugar can spike and crash like a rollercoaster — and that crash, not the pastry itself, is what's driving your 3 p.m. slump, your 9 p.m. cookie craving, and maybe even your skin breakouts. That's the provocation at the heart of Jessie Inchauspé's *Glucose Revolution*: the food you eat matters less than most diet books claim. The *order* and *combination* in which you eat it matters more.

## About the Book

Jessie Inchauspé is a French biochemist — not a doctor, dietitian, or clinical researcher, a distinction worth keeping in mind — who studied mathematics at King's College London before earning a master's in biochemistry and spending five years working in genetics in Silicon Valley. Her own turning point came after she broke her back at 19 and spent years fighting the mental-health fallout; wearing a continuous glucose monitor (CGM), she noticed her mood tracked almost exactly with how "spiky" or "flat" her blood sugar was that day.

Published in 2022, *Glucose Revolution* turned that personal observation into a bestselling wellness franchise — over 3 million copies sold across more than 40 languages — and launched Inchauspé's "Glucose Goddess" platform of hacks, supplements, and social-media graphics.

## The Central Argument

Inchauspé's thesis is disarmingly simple: it isn't glucose itself that's the enemy — glucose is your body's primary fuel — it's the *spike*, the sharp rise-and-crash pattern that happens when sugar and refined carbs hit your bloodstream too fast. She argues that chronic, repeated glucose spikes, even in people who are not diabetic and never will be, quietly drive fatigue, cravings, mood swings, poor sleep, skin problems, and — over years — insulin resistance and inflammation.

What makes the book's angle land with readers is that it isn't a diet. There's no calorie counting, no food eliminated outright. Instead, the book is organized in three parts: what glucose spikes are, why they're harmful, and — the part everyone actually reads for — ten low-effort "hacks" for flattening your glucose curve without changing what you eat.

## Key Ideas & Insights

### The "Clothes on Your Carbs" Rule

Inchauspé's most-quoted framework: pair any carbohydrate with fiber, protein, or fat, and those macronutrients act like "clothes" that slow how fast glucose is absorbed into your bloodstream. A slice of bread eaten alone spikes you hard; the same slice with butter and eggs barely moves the needle. This is the rule she returns to again and again — you're not removing carbs, you're insulating them.

### Eat in the Right Order

One of the book's most cited findings: eating in the sequence vegetables → protein and fat → starches and sugars, rather than mixing everything or eating carbs first, can cut the resulting glucose spike by more than 70%. It's the same plate, same portions — just resequenced. This is the hack readers report is easiest to actually keep doing, because it requires no new shopping list, just a different first bite.

### Savory Beats Sweet at Breakfast

Inchauspé calls breakfast the most consequential meal for glucose stability, because a sweet, high-carb breakfast (cereal, juice, pastries) sets a spike-prone pattern for the rest of the day — what she calls "glucose momentum." Swapping in a savory, protein-forward breakfast, she argues, flattens not just that one spike but subsequent meals too.

### The Vinegar Trick

A specific, testable hack: drinking a tablespoon of vinegar diluted in water before a carb-heavy meal. Inchauspé cites research showing this can blunt the resulting glucose spike by roughly 8–30%, and that vinegar taken before meals over a three-month period has been linked to reduced visceral fat and smaller waist measurements. It's the kind of concrete, cheap intervention that made the book shareable — one weird trick, backed by an actual (if small) study.

### Move After You Eat

The tenth hack is the least glamorous and probably the best-supported: any movement after a meal — a ten-minute walk, calf raises, light housework — measurably reduces the glucose and insulin response to that meal. Unlike some of the book's flashier claims, this one is uncontroversial and echoed across mainstream nutrition science, not just Inchauspé's own framework.

## Memorable Takeaways

- Spikes, not sugar itself, are the real target — flatten the curve rather than eliminate the food
- Food order changes your body's response to identical food — veggies and protein before carbs
- Carbs eaten "naked" (alone, on an empty stomach) hit harder than carbs eaten "dressed"
- A savory breakfast sets a calmer glucose pattern for the whole day
- A tablespoon of vinegar before a carb-heavy meal is a low-cost, low-effort spike-blunter
- Ten minutes of movement after eating meaningfully lowers your post-meal glucose and insulin response
- You don't need to adopt all ten hacks — pick the one or two that fit your life and stack from there

## Who Should Read This

This book is for the person who has tried and abandoned restrictive diets and wants a low-friction, "add this, don't remove that" framework instead — especially anyone already curious about energy crashes, sugar cravings, or midday fatigue and willing to experiment with meal sequencing. It will also appeal to readers who like data and self-tracking; Inchauspé's CGM-driven personal story gives the book a quantified-self flavor.

It's a poor fit for readers who want peer-reviewed rigor over anecdote — nutrition scientists have pointed out that the book leans on a thin, sometimes duplicated citation base and that the health risks of *normal, non-diabetic* glucose fluctuation are less settled than the book implies. If you need a source to be conservative in its claims, this isn't that source.

## Final Verdict

*Glucose Revolution*'s greatest strength is translation: it takes a real, useful piece of nutrition science — that meal composition and sequencing affect blood sugar response — and turns it into specific, testable, almost gamified actions anyone can try tonight. Its real weakness is overreach: the leap from "glucose spikes are measurable" to "glucose spikes are secretly behind most of your health and mood problems" outruns the evidence Inchauspé cites, and the book's commercial afterlife (supplements, an app, a growing hack-industrial complex) should make readers a little more skeptical, not less. Strip away the hype, though, and its lasting contribution is genuinely useful: it gave millions of people a free, physiologically grounded reason to eat their vegetables first.
